# Requirements and modeling workshops

<KimballRef chapters={[17, 18]} techniques={['Gather business requirements and data realities', 'Collaborative dimensional modeling workshops']}>
Chapter 17 places modeling inside the overall Kimball Lifecycle; Chapter 18 is a practical guide to
running the design process: who attends, what to prepare, and what to produce.
</KimballRef>

Dimensional models aren't designed by data engineers alone at a whiteboard. Kimball's method balances two
inputs:

| Business requirements | Data realities |
|---|---|
| What decisions do people make? | What do the source systems actually capture? |
| What do they measure, and how do they describe it? | At what grain? With what quality? |
| What questions do they ask that they can't answer today? | Are the keys stable? Is history kept? |

A model that ignores requirements is unused; a model that ignores data realities is unbuildable.

## Gathering requirements

Interview business people about **their work, not the data warehouse**. Useful prompts:

- "Walk me through what you review each week. What do you do when a number looks wrong?"
- "How do you know if this week was good or bad? Compared to what?"
- "Which groupings do you use: regions? categories? customer segments? Where do they come from?"
- "What do you have to do by hand in spreadsheets today?"

Listen for **verbs** (business processes), **nouns** (dimensions), **numbers** (facts) and **"by"** phrases
("sales *by* region *by* month"): those are dimension attributes.

## Profiling the data realities

Before the workshop, profile the candidate sources. In dbt that means writing staging models and quick
exploratory queries:

```sql
-- Is the natural key really unique? Does it ever change?
select sku, count(*) from raw.products group by sku having count(*) > 1;

-- What's the real grain of the POS feed?
select transaction_id, line_number, count(*)
from raw.pos_transactions group by all having count(*) > 1;

-- How often do attributes change? (drives SCD choices)
select attribute_name, count(*) from raw.product_changes group by 1;
```

Turn the discoveries into **staging-layer tests** (`unique`, `not_null`, `relationships`, `accepted_values`) so
the assumptions keep being checked; see `models/staging/cascade/_cascade__models.yml` in the lab.

## The workshop

Kimball recommends **collaborative modeling workshops**: business experts and modelers in the same room,
working process by process.

1. **High-level model (bubble chart).** For one business process, sketch the fact table in the middle and the
   candidate dimensions around it. Agree on the grain *first*.
2. **Detailed design.** For each table, list every column: name, description, sample values, source,
   SCD type for each attribute, and transformation rules. Kimball uses a spreadsheet-based "detailed dimensional
   design worksheet".
3. **Issues log.** Record every open question (definitions, data gaps, conflicts) with an owner.
4. **Review and validate** with a broader group, then iterate.

## Documentation in dbt

dbt gives you a natural home for the detailed design: model and column descriptions in YAML, published with
`dbt docs`. A useful convention is to record the **four-step decisions** in each fact model's header, and the
**SCD type** for each dimension column in its description:

```yaml
- name: dim_product
  description: Conformed product dimension. Type 2 on brand, category, department, package size.
  columns:
    - name: category
      description: "Merchandise category. SCD Type 2: history preserved."
    - name: product_name
      description: "Product name. SCD Type 1: overwritten (corrections only)."
```

## Pitfalls

- **Asking "what data do you want?"** You'll get a list of every field in the source. Ask about
  decisions and measurements instead.
- **Designing for one report.** Ask which other teams use the same process; that's where conformed dimensions
  come from (see [The bus matrix](/bus/bus-matrix)).
- **Skipping data profiling.** Grain surprises (duplicate lines, reused ticket numbers) are much cheaper to find
  before the model is built.
